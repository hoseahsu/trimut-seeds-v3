A technician blocks outlets in a liquid distribution manifold.

Marek wants to water his only planter. Unfortunately, he has a very poor distribution manifold that was designed for n planters and so it looks like a conduit with n outlets. Marek can only use the water that flows from the first outlet.

Marek can block some of the outlets, and then pour A liters of water into the conduit. After that, the water will flow out from the non-blocked outlets proportionally to their sizes s_1, s_2, …, s_n. In other words, if the sum of sizes of non-blocked outlets is S, and the i-th outlet is not blocked, (s_i ⋅ A)/(S) liters of water will flow out of it.

What is the minimum number of outlets Marek should block to make at least B liters of water flow out of the first outlet?

Input

The first line contains three integers n, A, B (1 ≤ n ≤ 100 000, 1 ≤ B ≤ A ≤ 10^4) — the number of outlets, the volume of water Marek will pour into the system, and the volume he wants to get out of the first outlet.

The second line contains n integers s_1, s_2, …, s_n (1 ≤ s_i ≤ 10^4) — the sizes of the outlets.

Output

Print a single integer — the number of outlets Marek should block.

Examples

Input

4 10 3
2 2 2 2


Output

1


Input

4 80 20
3 2 1 4


Output

0


Input

5 10 10
1000 1 1 1 1


Output

4

Note

In the first example Marek should block at least one outlet. After that, (10 ⋅ 2)/(6) ≈ 3.333 liters of water will flow out of the first outlet, and that suits Marek.

In the second example even without blocking any outlet, (80 ⋅ 3)/(10) = 24 liters will flow out of the first outlet, that is not less than 20.

In the third example Marek has to block all outlets except the first to make all water flow out of the first outlet.
